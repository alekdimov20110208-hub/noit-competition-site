# Core Interview Synthesis

## Purpose

This document provides a consistent structure for comparing the four completed core validation interviews: PILOT-A1, INT-A2, INT-A3, and INT-A4. The planned core interview phase is complete for the current stage. INT-A5 is reserved for targeted follow-up only if market research or later prototype testing reveals a specific evidence gap; it is not required before market research. The synthesis must not be used to treat isolated reports as general facts or to authorize implementation prematurely.

## Current hypothesis under test

The INT-A3-informed hypothesis is a private, mobile-friendly organizer cockpit for martial-arts seminars, built around reusable seminar playbooks, a long-range timeline, clear responsibility and completion confirmation, private contingency planning, promotion and merchandise checkpoints, guest logistics, and post-event learning, while urgent communication remains in existing phone and chat tools. The organizer keeps the complete picture, while collaborators receive only the tasks and confirmations relevant to them.

INT-A4 introduces a competing hypothesis focused on first-time or infrequent seminar organizers. Neither primary-user segment is selected. Both remain provisional hypotheses to test, not validated concepts or selected final solutions.

## Competing primary-user hypotheses

### Candidate segment A — experienced chief organizers of larger or more complex seminars

- **[RI]** Possible value: A private organizer cockpit for long-range planning, critical deadlines, private contingencies, knowledge transfer, complex international guest logistics, promotion, and merchandise tracking.
- **[PR]** Main supporting evidence: INT-A3 reports a complex recurring international seminar whose complete picture and activation logic remained concentrated in its chief organizer.
- **[PO]** Main contradiction: INT-A4 suggests that an experienced organizer in a mature recurring team may not use a formal planning application once the process has become habitual.

### Candidate segment B — first-time or infrequent martial-arts seminar organizers

- **[RI]** Possible value: A guided reusable playbook that explains what must be done and why, supports onboarding, reduces common omissions, provides an adaptable timeline, and enables simple delegation.
- **[PO]** Main supporting evidence: INT-A4 identifies first-time or rare seminar hosts as more plausible users.
- **[PO]** Additional supporting evidence: PILOT-A1 preferred specialization, and INT-A2 favored reusable specialized templates.
- **[PR]** Main supporting evidence: INT-A3 reports that another martial-arts group sought experienced organizational advice.
- **[RI]** Main uncertainty: No actual first-time seminar organizer has yet been interviewed or observed.

Neither segment is selected. Competitor and market research must examine both before prototype scope is chosen.

- **[OE] Observed evidence** — behavior or an artifact directly observed during research. No such evidence is currently recorded for PILOT-A1, INT-A2, INT-A3, or INT-A4.

## Decision gate

This gate decides only whether the evidence supports moving to the next validation stage. The planned core interview phase is complete for the current stage; INT-A5 is reserved for targeted follow-up only if market research or later prototype testing reveals a specific evidence gap. The gate does not authorize application code, architecture, prototyping, or implementation. Broader validation requirements remain applicable.

**Current overall status: `REVISE / MOVE TO MARKET RESEARCH`.** Four validation interviews are complete. The specialized martial-arts seminar domain has enough evidence to justify competitor and market research, but the primary user remains unresolved between experienced chief organizers of complex seminars and first-time or infrequent organizers. Development is not authorized. Market research must compare current tools and competing solutions before prototype scope is selected. A targeted additional interview may still be added if a specific gap emerges. No prototype has been tested, and willingness to pay remains untested.

### PROCEED

Proceed to a bounded low-fidelity validation stage only when:

- at least three completed core interviews contain a specific relevant past incident with an observable consequence;
- a coherent primary user and recurring event workflow are supported by more than one interview;
- the proposed direction addresses a gap not already handled sufficiently by current tools;
- at least two participants identify conditions under which they would use the specialized approach;
- a plausible person responsible for maintaining the information is identified;
- major counter-evidence and adoption barriers have a testable response;
- the decision selects a narrow hypothesis, not a generic event-management platform.

### REVISE

Revise the problem, audience, or value proposition when:

- the coordination problem repeats, but evidence supports different event categories or users;
- incidents are credible, but existing tools appear sufficient for most participants;
- maintenance burden, duplicated updates, or unclear ownership remain unresolved;
- participants value templates, reminders, or guided risk checks but not the current mechanism;
- supporting and contradicting evidence are too balanced for a defensible direction.

Define one revision and test it with new evidence before reconsidering `PROCEED`.

### KILL

Kill the current product hypothesis when:

- fewer than three completed core interviews provide a specific relevant past incident;
- reported consequences are consistently minor and do not justify a separate product;
- most participants report that current tools are sufficient and cannot identify differentiated value;
- no credible primary user or maintainer emerges;
- likely users reject the required update effort even for the narrowest useful workflow;
- the remaining concept is only generic task management, a thin AI wrapper, or a duplicate of existing tools.

Killing the current hypothesis does not erase valid evidence about the broader coordination problem; it prevents unsupported implementation of this solution.
