# Core Interview Synthesis

## Purpose

This document provides a consistent structure for comparing the five core validation interviews: PILOT-A1, INT-A2, INT-A3, INT-A4, and INT-A5. It is intended to support an evidence-based decision after all five interviews are complete. It must not be used to treat isolated reports as general facts or to authorize implementation prematurely.

## Current hypothesis under test

The current provisional hypothesis is a lightweight, mobile-friendly, reusable playbook for small martial-arts seminar organizing teams that helps them manage task ownership, deadlines, confirmations, simple dependencies, fallback people or resources, and guided checks for commonly forgotten risks, while allowing urgent communication to remain in existing chat and phone channels.

This is a hypothesis to test, not a validated concept or selected final solution.

## Evidence labels

- **[OE] Observed evidence** — behavior or an artifact directly observed during research. No such evidence is currently recorded for PILOT-A1 or INT-A2.
- **[PR] Participant report** — the participant's account of a past process, incident, or behavior.
- **[PO] Participant opinion** — the participant's preference, judgment, prediction, or reaction to a concept.
- **[RI] Researcher inference** — an interpretation derived from the interview records; it remains a hypothesis until separately tested.
- **Pending interview** — no evidence has been collected for that participant code.

## Decision gate

This five-interview gate decides only whether a narrow product hypothesis is ready for the next validation stage. It does not authorize application code, architecture, or full implementation. Broader validation requirements remain applicable.

### PROCEED

Proceed to a bounded low-fidelity validation stage only when:

- at least three of the five interviews contain a specific relevant past incident with an observable consequence;
- a coherent primary user and recurring event workflow are supported by more than one interview;
- the proposed direction addresses a gap not already handled sufficiently by current tools;
- at least two participants identify conditions under which they would use the specialized approach;
- a plausible person responsible for maintaining the information is identified;
- major counter-evidence and adoption barriers have a testable response;
- the decision selects a narrow hypothesis, not a generic event-management platform.

### REVISE

Revise the problem, audience, or value proposition when:

- the coordination problem repeats, but evidence supports different event categories or users;
- incidents are credible, but existing tools appear sufficient for most participants;
- maintenance burden, duplicated updates, or unclear ownership remain unresolved;
- participants value templates, reminders, or guided risk checks but not the current mechanism;
- supporting and contradicting evidence are too balanced for a defensible direction.

Define one revision and test it with new evidence before reconsidering `PROCEED`.

### KILL

Kill the current product hypothesis when:

- fewer than three of the five interviews provide a specific relevant past incident;
- reported consequences are consistently minor and do not justify a separate product;
- most participants report that current tools are sufficient and cannot identify differentiated value;
- no credible primary user or maintainer emerges;
- likely users reject the required update effort even for the narrowest useful workflow;
- the remaining concept is only generic task management, a thin AI wrapper, or a duplicate of existing tools.

Killing the current hypothesis does not erase valid evidence about the broader coordination problem; it prevents unsupported implementation of this solution.

## Questions still unanswered

1. Which event category has the strongest repeated evidence: martial-arts seminars, school theatre productions, or another narrow workflow?
2. Who creates and maintains the shared plan, and at which moments is updating it realistic?
3. Which information repeats across events and can be reused safely as a template?
4. Which details are most often forgotten, and what consequences follow?
5. Are guided risk prompts more valuable than explicit what-if simulation?
6. Which reminders reduce organizer effort without creating notification fatigue?
7. What must work on a phone during preparation and on the event day?
8. What differentiated value would justify use alongside or instead of chat, calls, and shared documents?
9. Would a new team adopt the system more readily than an established team?
10. What setup and maintenance time is acceptable for the likely primary user?
11. Are confirmations, payments, travel, venue, equipment, or fallback arrangements the most valuable specialized domain?
12. Does repeated use of prior event plans create enough value to drive return usage?
