# ReadyGraph problem-validation interview guide

## Status and purpose

ReadyGraph is a provisional concept. The problem, target workflow, and proposed value are hypotheses until supported by real interviews and prototype tests.

These interviews investigate how small events are currently prepared, what has actually gone wrong, and whether organizers need a clearer way to represent dependencies. They are not sales conversations and must not be used to persuade participants that ReadyGraph is useful.

## Interview rules

- Interview 6-10 people, aiming for representation from all three audiences.
- Ask about one real event at a time, preferably a recent one.
- Ask for concrete examples, sequences, artifacts, and consequences.
- Do not mention ReadyGraph, dependency graphs, readiness scores, or AI before completing the ten questions.
- Do not convert opinions into facts. Record exact words when they matter.
- Ask permission before recording audio, viewing chats or documents, or retaining any artifact.
- Do not copy personal, confidential, or unnecessary information into the notes.
- Do not promise legal, safety, or operational compliance.
- Silence is acceptable. Do not complete the participant's answer.

Useful neutral probes:

- "Can you walk me through what happened?"
- "What happened next?"
- "Where was that recorded?"
- "Who noticed it, and when?"
- "Do you remember another example?"
- "May I see a blank or anonymized example?"

Avoid leading prompts such as:

- "Would a dependency graph have prevented that?"
- "Isn't chat too chaotic?"
- "Would you use a tool that solves this?"
- "How useful would ReadyGraph be?"

## Audience A: aikido instructors and club organizers

1. **Think about the most recent seminar, grading, demonstration, or club event you helped organize. How was the preparation started and carried through to the event day?**  
   Purpose: reconstruct the real process rather than an idealized process.

2. **What information did you need during preparation, and where was each type of information kept?**  
   Probe neutrally for chats, documents, spreadsheets, calendars, paper notes, checklists, and memory only if the participant does not mention storage.

3. **Who was involved in preparing the event, and how did people learn what they were responsible for?**  
   Purpose: understand roles, handoffs, ownership, and informal coordination.

4. **Tell me about a past event where a person, resource, confirmation, or prerequisite was missing or discovered later than expected.**  
   If no example is recalled, ask: "Has that ever happened in a smaller way?"

5. **What were the consequences of that omission, and what did people do in response?**  
   Probe for delay, extra work, cancellation, reduced quality, confusion, cost, or no meaningful consequence without suggesting that one must have occurred.

6. **How often do preparation problems of this kind occur across the events you organize?**  
   Ask for a concrete denominator where possible: per event, per season, or per year. Do not offer a frequency range first.

7. **How do you currently check that one activity is not waiting on another person, resource, decision, or confirmation?**  
   Purpose: discover existing dependency-management behavior.

8. **Which current tools or methods work well, and which create extra work or feel more complex than the event requires?**  
   Do not assume the tools are too complex; "none" is a valid answer.

9. **Before an event, do you consider what would happen if a key person or resource became unavailable? If so, how is that considered or recorded?**  
   Purpose: examine present contingency planning without introducing the proposed solution.

10. **For an event similar to the one described, under what circumstances, if any, would spending 10-15 minutes building a structured plan be worthwhile? When would it not be worthwhile?**  
    Purpose: test the setup-cost hypothesis without asking for commitment to ReadyGraph.

## Audience B: teachers and school-event organizers

1. **Please choose a recent school event you helped organize and describe the preparation from the first decision to the event itself.**

2. **What information was needed, and where was it stored or discussed during preparation?**  
   Probe only when needed for email, school platforms, chats, shared documents, spreadsheets, checklists, and paper.

3. **How were responsibilities divided among teachers, students, administration, and any external participants?**

4. **Describe a real event where a required person, room, resource, approval, confirmation, or preceding task was missed or became available late.**

5. **What happened because of that omission, including any workaround or change to the event?**

6. **Across the events you know, how often do comparable preparation problems occur?**  
   Ask what records or memories support the estimate.

7. **How do organizers currently identify tasks that cannot begin until another action or approval is complete?**

8. **What works well in the tools you currently use, and what, if anything, feels unnecessarily difficult or complex?**

9. **How are backup arrangements handled when a room, responsible person, equipment item, or approval might not be available?**

10. **When would it be reasonable to spend 10-15 minutes representing an event as a structured plan, and when would a normal checklist be sufficient?**

## Audience C: students who help organize activities

1. **Think of a recent activity, celebration, club event, competition, campaign, or presentation you helped organize. What did you personally do from the beginning to the end?**

2. **Where did you look for dates, responsibilities, decisions, and updates while preparing?**  
   Probe only when needed for group chats, documents, spreadsheets, school platforms, checklists, and memory.

3. **How did you know what you were expected to do and what other people needed to finish first?**

4. **Tell me about a time when someone, something, a confirmation, or an earlier task was missing or late during preparation.**

5. **What effect did that have on you, the other organizers, or the activity?**

6. **How often have you seen similar problems when helping with activities?**  
   Ask for examples rather than accepting "often" or "rarely" without context.

7. **How did the group check whether all important work, people, and materials were ready?**

8. **Which parts of the current tools or methods helped you, and which parts, if any, were confusing or took unnecessary effort?**

9. **Did the group discuss backup options before the activity? How were those options communicated or remembered?**

10. **For an activity like this, when would you be willing to spend 10-15 minutes helping build a structured plan, and what would make that effort not worthwhile?**

## Closing after question 10

Only after the problem interview is complete, the interviewer may say:

> We are considering a prototype that represents an event as connected people, resources, tasks, and backup options, then lets an organizer explore what is affected when one item becomes unavailable. The concept is not yet validated or implemented.

Then ask only these concept-test questions, keeping them separate from problem evidence:

1. What is unclear in that description?
2. Which part, if any, relates to the process you described?
3. What would make the approach unsuitable for your events?
4. Would you be willing to test a paper prototype in a separate session?

Answers after the concept is disclosed are **concept reactions**, not evidence that the original problem exists.
