## Participant and event context

- **[PR]** The participant has extensive practical experience as the chief organizer of recurring international martial-arts seminars.
- **[PR]** The participant was responsible for the overall plan, information, decisions, delegation, logistics, promotion, and contingencies.
- **[PR]** The recent seminar discussed involved a returning international instructor, a new international instructor, guests from multiple countries, and approximately one hundred participants.
- **[PR]** The event involved a significant budget covering instructor costs, travel, and accommodation. The confidential exact figure is intentionally excluded.
- **[PR]** Planning covered travel, accommodation, local transport, the training schedule, registration, finances, promotion, merchandise, hospitality, and support for international visitors.
- **[PR]** Multiple helpers performed assigned work, while the complete organizational picture remained concentrated in the chief organizer.
- **[PR]** Visitors considered the seminar successful.
- **[PR]** The organizer judged several internal parts of the organization much more critically.

## Long preparation timeline

- **[PR]** Initial discussions and hierarchy-related approval began long before the event.
- **[PR]** A formal invitation followed preliminary approval.
- **[PR]** No public announcement was made before final instructor confirmation.
- **[PR]** The seminar was announced approximately one year before the event.
- **[PR]** Flights were commonly arranged several months before the event.
- **[PR]** Preparation for a summer seminar should ideally begin around November or December.
- **[PR]** Promotion increased gradually and became more intensive several months before the seminar.
- **[PR]** Promotion for the latest seminar began later than the participant considered appropriate.
- **[RI]** The reported process supports testing a long-range seminar timeline rather than treating seminar preparation as a short task list.

## Reusable organizational model

- **[PR]** The participant described a seminar as requiring a reusable model or scheme with stable reference points.
- **[PR]** Repeated elements included instructor invitation and confirmation, travel, arrival and departure, local transport, accommodation, food and hospitality, venue, training schedule, registration, payments, participant information, promotion, merchandise, staffing, and support for international visitors.
- **[PR]** The model expands or contracts according to expected attendance, promotion reach, the number of international guests, venue capacity, and the number of required helpers.
- **[PR]** Another martial-arts group had asked the participant for advice on a substantial part of its own seminar preparation.
- **[RI]** The request for advice is participant-reported evidence that experienced organizational know-how may be valuable to less experienced organizers. It does not establish demand for software or a general market pattern.

## Information concentration and organizer burden

- **[PR]** The participant estimated that approximately 98% of the information, overall coordination, decisions, and timely activation of work depended on the chief organizer.
- **[PR]** The participant distinguished this concentration from physical execution: helpers performed much of the assigned work, so the chief organizer completed a smaller share of all physical tasks.
- **[PR]** The participant believed the seminar could continue operationally without the chief organizer because helpers understood their assigned work and a recurring model existed.
- **[PR]** Transferring the complete organizational picture would require another person to participate in the process and learn the model over time.
- **[RI]** There is an unresolved tension between operational delegation and concentration of the complete picture and activation logic in one person.

## Task assignment and completion tracking

- **[PR]** Tasks were assigned mainly orally.
- **[PR]** People were selected according to personal trust and experience.
- **[PR]** Completion was often assumed after a task had been assigned to a reliable person.
- **[PR]** There was no systematic written completion-confirmation workflow.
- **[PR]** The participant indicated that task distribution should be written for the next seminar.
- **[PR]** For time-sensitive guest transport, helpers were briefed in advance and each person knew whom to collect and when.
- **[PR]** The chief organizer still called or messaged helpers to confirm that transport was proceeding correctly.
- **[PR]** The participant described the chief organizer as the reminder and proactive checking point.
- **[RI]** This supports further testing of written responsibility, deadlines, lightweight confirmations, selective reminders, and organizer visibility.
- **[RI]** The interview does not establish a request for automated notifications.

## Existing tools and communication

- **[PR]** The organizer used telephone calls, chat, social networks, Viber for many Bulgarian contacts, WhatsApp for many international contacts, and paper lists.
- **[PR]** The responsible person maintained a duplicated paper financial record.
- **[PR]** The organizer team did not use a shared planning document, shared task table, or shared calendar.
- **[PO]** Phone and chat were valued because they provide immediate feedback.
- **[RI]** A proposed product should preserve urgent communication through phone, Viber, and WhatsApp rather than attempting to replace it.

## Dependencies

- **[PR]** The participant initially said that critical dependencies should be prevented through preparation and therefore did not describe the seminar as containing major unresolved dependencies.
- **[PR]** Final instructor confirmation preceded public announcement.
- **[PR]** Instructor travel information preceded transport planning, and arrival times preceded pickup.
- **[PR]** Promotion level affected attendance, and attendance affected required staffing.
- **[PR]** Venue capacity affected venue selection.
- **[PR]** Schedule approval preceded publication.
- **[PR]** Instructor availability affected who taught.
- **[PR]** Guest information affected hospitality planning.
- **[RI]** Good preparation may make operational dependencies feel invisible to an experienced organizer even though prerequisite relationships remain present.
- **[RI]** INT-A3 does not validate a complex dependency graph as a desired feature.

## Contingency planning

- **[PR]** Backup plans existed mainly in the chief organizer's memory.
- **[PO]** The participant believed contingency information should be visible only to the organizer and possibly one or two trusted people, rather than the complete team.
- **[RI]** This supports testing private contingency notes, restricted visibility, backup owners or substitutes, and guided risk questions.
- **[RI]** It does not support making every risk visible to every collaborator.

## Concrete past disruption: delayed guest instructor

| Element | Grounded finding |
|---|---|
| Planned element | **[PR]** A guest instructor was expected to teach according to the public program. |
| Disruption | **[PR]** The instructor arrived approximately one day later than planned. |
| Preparation | **[PR]** The chief organizer had anticipated a possible travel delay and had included himself among the seminar instructors. |
| Fallback | **[PR]** The chief organizer substituted for the delayed instructor. |
| Consequence | **[PR]** The public schedule remained broadly stable, and participants did not experience the situation as a major breakdown. |

- **[RI]** This is strong participant-reported evidence for preplanned substitution, fallback ownership, protecting a published schedule, and asking risk questions before an event.
- **[RI]** It is not proof that automatic dependency simulation is necessary.

## Main weaknesses and failures

- **[PR]** The participant identified promotion and merchandise as the weakest areas.
- **[PR]** Promotion started later than the participant considered ideal, and seminar advertising was judged weak.
- **[PR]** Merchandise planning was judged weak.
- **[PR]** Roughly one quarter of an ordered towel quantity was sold.
- **[PO]** Some promotional items may have been more appropriate as gifts.
- **[PR]** A planned interview did not happen because of timing and insufficient journalistic preparation.
- **[PR]** Some promotional work did not have clearly tracked deadlines.
- **[PR]** Video was not recorded systematically for use in future promotion.
- **[RI]** A seminar can appear successful to visitors while still containing internal planning failures that could be recorded and used for later improvement.

## Venue, schedule, and guest logistics

- **[PO]** The participant considered the schedule especially critical.
- **[PO]** The published program was treated as a commitment to attendees and should change only as a last resort.
- **[PR]** The venue was less risky for this event because a default venue was available.
- **[PR]** Larger future attendance could require alternative venues.
- **[PR]** The process considered a default venue, checked alternatives, capacity, preparation time before and after sessions, accommodation, transport, arrival and departure details, guest preferences, rest periods, and plans for new guests whose preferences were not yet known.
- **[PR]** Some guests stayed at the training venue, increasing operational complexity.

## Concept reaction after disclosure

The following evidence was recorded after the specialized product direction was explained. It is concept-reaction evidence, not proof that the pre-concept problem exists or that adoption will occur.

- **[PO]** The participant reacted positively to a specialized concept.
- **[PO]** The system could function as the private know-how and working instrument of the chief organizer or promoter.
- **[PO]** The system should strengthen the organizer's personal method.
- **[PO]** The participant was skeptical about giving the complete organizational playbook directly to every organizer.
- **[PO]** The human organizer's personal approach remained essential.
- **[PO]** The participant suggested testing the system alongside the organization of an upcoming seminar.
- **[PO]** A possible workflow could use green and red status indicators to help reveal neglected areas such as promotion.
- **[RI]** These statements are willingness signals and design opinions, not behavioral proof of adoption or willingness to pay.
- **[RI]** A consulting or promoter-led business remains only one possible interpretation and is not selected by this interview.

## Revised product hypothesis

**[RI]** INT-A3 supports revising the hypothesis under test toward:

> A private, mobile-friendly organizer cockpit for martial-arts seminars, built around reusable seminar playbooks, a long-range timeline, clear responsibility and completion confirmation, private contingency planning, promotion and merchandise checkpoints, guest logistics, and post-event learning, while urgent communication remains in existing phone and chat tools.

**[RI]** The team-facing component should remain lightweight: the organizer keeps the complete picture, collaborators receive only relevant tasks and confirmation requests, and private risk information need not be shared with everyone.

This is a revised hypothesis, not a validated or final product decision.

- deadlines and completion confirmation;
- red/green status;

- arrival, departure, and transport planning;
- schedule approval and schedule protection;
- promotion checkpoints;
- merchandise planning;
- venue alternatives;
- post-event lessons and reusable improvements;
- mobile usability;
- limited collaborator views.

## Features not validated by INT-A3

- a general-purpose event platform;
- replacing chat and telephone communication;
- requiring every helper to use a complex new application;
- public visibility of every contingency;
- a complex visual dependency graph;
- automatic simulation as the core value;
- a fully self-service playbook sold unchanged to every organizer;
- willingness to pay;
- frequency of use across enough independent organizations.

## Contradictions and tensions

1. **[PR]** The participant said the event could continue without the chief organizer, while also estimating that approximately 98% of information, coordination, decisions, and timely activation depended on that person.
2. **[PR]** The participant said meaningful dependencies were prevented in advance, while the described workflow contained multiple managed prerequisite relationships.
3. **[PR]** The participant initially said no backup plan had to be used, while also describing substitution for a delayed instructor.
4. **[PR]** Visitors considered the organization excellent, while the organizer considered some internal areas poor.

- **[RI]** An experienced organizer may not label a successfully absorbed disruption as a failure or backup-plan activation. This interpretation was not stated by the participant and requires further testing.

## Interview-level decision

`REVISE`

The target niche, repeatable workflow, organizer information concentration, written ownership, lightweight confirmations, private contingencies, and long-range planning received strong participant-level support. The earlier assumption of a broadly shared team platform should be revised toward a private organizer cockpit with lightweight collaborator interaction.

More independent interviews are required. INT-A3 does not authorize an overall `PROCEED` decision, application development, or final product selection.
