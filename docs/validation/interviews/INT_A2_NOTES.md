## Participant context

The participant had limited involvement in the full organization of the latest martial-arts seminar. For that reason, the main detailed example shifted to small music and party events where the participant had acted as a team leader, directly divided the work, and assigned responsibilities.

## How the events were organized

- Preparation usually began around one month before an event.
- Planning covered the venue, date, event concept, promotion, printed materials, decoration, sound equipment, DJs, transportation, entrance arrangements, and financial arrangements with the venue.
- The core team consisted of approximately three main unpaid organizers and one paid social-media contributor.
- Responsibilities were assigned according to each person's skills and interests.
- The team used meetings, a shared Google Drive document, task checkboxes, and deadlines.

## Information and communication

- Live meetings were preferred for discussion.
- Messenger or another group chat was used for ongoing communication.
- Google Drive and shared documents held structured information.
- At the beginning, too much information existed only in the main organizer's memory.
- The participant explicitly described dependence on one person's memory as a mistake.
- Messages in a chat can become difficult to find later.
- Some information has to be copied from chat into a more permanent document.
- Even when a shared document exists, people may fail to open or check it.

## Concrete past incidents

### 1. Missing QR code on printed promotional materials

Printed promotional materials were produced without a planned QR code. The team then had to print QR codes separately, cut them out, and attach them manually as stickers. The omitted detail caused avoidable additional work after printing.

### 2. Substantially higher attendance than expected

One event received substantially more visitors than expected. This created problems at the entrance, at the bar, and inside the venue. The organizers had to redistribute responsibilities quickly while the event was in progress.

### 3. Forgotten technical equipment

Important technical equipment was forgotten. Shortly before the event, an urgent taxi trip was required to retrieve it.

### 4. Work completed only after repeated reminders

Some team members did not complete assigned work without repeated reminders from the main organizer. This increased the organizer's coordination burden and concentrated responsibility in one person.

### 5. Last-minute seminar changes

In martial-arts seminar organization, late changes can include delayed arrivals, changes in transport, food stops, or changes to the programme caused by illness. These changes require rapid communication and adjustment of the current plan.

## Dependencies

- Dependencies between activities were real and were sometimes written directly in the task document.
- One example was that the promotional design had to be completed before materials could be printed.
- Deadlines were used to express and manage these dependencies.
- The participant believed that a capable organizer who created the full plan often already understands the consequences of a missing task or person.

## Current tools: strengths

- Live conversation is fast and was preferred for discussion.
- Google Drive makes structured information visible and accessible.
- Chats support quick communication, pinned messages, polls, and read indicators.
- Phone calls were preferred for urgent and simple corrections.

## Current tools: weaknesses

- Important messages can become buried in the chat history.
- Informal and work-related conversations become mixed together.
- People may ignore or fail to check shared documents.
- The central organizer may have to remind other people repeatedly.
- Introducing a new platform can duplicate information between conversations, chat, and the platform.

## Fallback plans

- Fallback people existed for entrance management and music.
- Fallback arrangements were often discussed verbally or in chat.
- Not every affected person always knew the fallback plan.
- In some situations, the problem itself forced an immediate switch to plan B.

## Acceptable planning time

- Approximately five minutes could be enough for a very rough outline.
- Ten to fifteen minutes could be acceptable for an initial plan.
- Around thirty minutes could produce a useful first version.
- A detailed plan could reasonably take up to one hour.
- Reusing the plan from a previous event would make later events faster to prepare.

## Reaction to the concept

### Potentially useful aspects

- Keeping central information in one place.
- Assigning responsibilities clearly.
- Making task status visible.
- Showing which later activities become delayed when an earlier activity is incomplete.

### Reservations and objections

- The participant immediately compared the concept with existing task-board and project-management tools.
- A new platform would need to provide something that existing tools do not already offer.
- The simulation feature might be useful for larger organizations but unnecessary for small teams.
- The deeper issue might be remembering to consider a risk, rather than calculating all of its consequences.
- Guided prompts such as “What happens if an important person is unavailable?” might be more valuable than requiring users to construct manual simulations.
- The participant would not replace a working Viber and Google Drive process without major advantages.
- Adoption might be easier when a new team starts using the tool from the beginning rather than when an established team has to replace its current process.

These are reactions to the proposed solution. They do not negate the reported coordination incidents.

## Strongest evidence supporting the problem

- Real details were missed during preparation.
- Omissions caused emergency extra work, including manual correction and urgent travel.
- Dependencies existed between tasks, deadlines, people, and event resources.
- Too much information was initially concentrated in one organizer's memory.
- The main organizer frequently had to remind others to complete work.
- Last-minute attendance, transport, programme, and staffing changes required rapid replanning.

## Strongest evidence against the current solution

- Existing tools already cover tasks, deadlines, shared information, and rapid communication.
- The participant did not personally need manual what-if simulations for small teams.
- Another platform would introduce training effort and duplicated updates.
- Urgent communication would still happen faster by phone or chat.

## Interview limitations

- Several interviewer questions contained examples or assumptions.
- Some questions were rephrased unclearly.
- The interviewer occasionally suggested who probably handled a problem.
- The concept discussion included some defensive explanation.
- The question about who would maintain the system was not fully explored.
- Prototype willingness and final consent were confirmed after the recorded main discussion.
- The automatic transcript contains errors, so these notes rely only on clear semantic meaning rather than uncertain wording.

## Preliminary interpretation

Within the scope of this interview, the evidence strongly validates the coordination problem but does not validate a generic event-management platform. It provides stronger support for reusable, specialized event templates, guided risk checks, clear ownership, deadlines, and reminders.

This remains evidence from one participant and must be assessed alongside additional interviews before a product direction is selected.
