# Interview INT-SATO Guide

## 1. Interview objective

This interview investigates how Aikido seminars involving a visiting instructor are organized, what information and confirmations the instructor needs, where communication or coordination problems occur, and how practical organizational knowledge is transferred between host dojos.

The interview examines Sato Sensei's perspective as an internationally experienced visiting instructor and senior Aikido leader. It does not assume that he is the local event administrator, that every local decision passes through him, or that he personally completes host-dojo tasks. Questions must distinguish decisions requiring the visiting instructor's confirmation from decisions handled independently by the host dojo.

The project is presented as research into how Aikido seminars and events are organized and how technology might make the process more effective. No finished application is claimed.

## 2. Interviewer rules

- Ask about real and recent examples.
- Do not rush through every question. Follow the strongest examples and omit lower-priority probes when necessary.
- Follow strong examples with:
  - "What happened next?"
  - "Who was involved?"
  - "What was the practical consequence?"
  - "How was the situation resolved?"
- Do not lead the participant toward the application.
- Separate observed behavior and participant reports from general advice or opinion.
- Do not record personal or unnecessary sensitive details.
- Request consent separately for anonymized written notes and audio recording.
- If audio recording is refused, continue only with permitted notes.
- Do not treat concept feedback as demand evidence.
- Do not assume that the visiting instructor performs local operational tasks.
- Do not assume that every organizational decision requires the visiting instructor's approval.
- Keep the discussion focused on the Aikido community unless the participant independently raises another context.

## 3. Opening script

Obtain consent for anonymized written notes separately. Then read:

> Dear Sato Sensei,
>
> Thank you very much for taking the time to speak with me today. I truly appreciate the opportunity to learn from your experience.
>
> As Getov Sensei mentioned, I am researching how Aikido seminars and events are organized and how technology might make the process more effective. At this stage, I am not presenting a finished application. My goal is first to understand the real organizational process, the responsibilities involved, the difficulties that occur, and the tools currently used within the Aikido community.
>
> I would like to ask you mainly about specific experiences and real examples. The conversation should take approximately thirty minutes.
>
> Would it be alright if I record the conversation only so that I can review your answers accurately afterward? The recording would not be shared with anyone.
>
> Does that sound alright?

If audio recording is refused, continue only if anonymized written notes are permitted and use:

> I will be taking some notes during our conversation so that I can review your answers accurately afterward.

Record written-notes consent and audio-recording consent separately as `yes`, `no`, or `not requested`. Raw audio and transcripts must remain outside Git.

## 4. Thirty-minute interview route

| Time | Focus |
|---|---|
| 0:00-3:00 | Opening and consent |
| 3:00-8:00 | Most recent seminar and required information |
| 8:00-13:00 | Communication, changes, and repeated clarification |
| 13:00-18:00 | Real problems and key-person dependency |
| 18:00-23:00 | Differences between hosts and transfer of knowledge |
| 23:00-26:00 | Existing tools and previous process changes |
| 26:00-28:30 | Neutral concept discussion |
| 28:30-30:00 | Missing topics, referrals, and closing |

Use this route as a priority guide, not as a requirement to read every probe. Specific past behavior takes priority over complete question coverage.

## 5. Core questions

### CORE 1. Most recent seminar

Could you tell me about the most recent seminar you taught as a visiting instructor?

Possible probes:

- Where did it take place?
- Who was your main contact from the host dojo?
- How far in advance did the planning begin?

### CORE 2. Initial and later information

Thinking about that seminar, what information did the host dojo initially send you, and what still had to be discussed afterward?

Possible probes:

- Was the information complete from the beginning?
- Did you have to request any additional details?
- How was the information sent?

### CORE 3. Matters requiring confirmation

Before a seminar, what information or decisions does the host dojo usually need to confirm with you?

Possible probes:

- Do you normally review the training schedule?
- Are there special requirements related to the seminar format, grading, demonstrations, or participants?
- Which details can the host dojo normally decide independently?

### CORE 4. Information needed before travel

What information do you personally need before traveling to teach a seminar, and when do you normally expect to receive it?

Possible probes:

- Do you receive one complete overview or several separate messages?
- Does any information frequently arrive later than it should?
- Have you ever travelled without having all the necessary details?

### CORE 5. Communication and current information

How do host dojos normally communicate seminar arrangements and later changes to you?

Possible probes:

- Email, messaging applications, calls, shared documents, or Zoom?
- Is there normally one central source of current information?
- How do you know that the version you received is still current?

### CORE 6. Recent corrections or changes

Thinking about a recent seminar, were there arrangements that had to be changed or corrected before the event?

Possible probes:

- What changed?
- Who noticed the issue?
- How was the correction communicated?
- Did it create additional work, delay, or confusion?

### CORE 7. Repeated explanations

Are there instructions or organizational details that you find yourself explaining repeatedly to different host dojos?

Possible probes:

- What is most often misunderstood or overlooked?
- Is that guidance documented anywhere?
- Why do the same questions continue to appear?

### CORE 8. Last problem affecting the instructor

Thinking about seminars you have taught recently, can you remember the last organizational problem that affected you directly as the visiting instructor?

Possible probes:

- What exactly happened?
- When did you first learn about it?
- Who had to resolve it?
- What was the practical consequence?

### CORE 9. Knowledge held by one person

Have you attended a seminar where most of the important organizational knowledge appeared to be held by one person?

Possible probes:

- How could you tell?
- What happened when other people needed information?
- Did the situation create delays, repeated questions, or pressure?

### CORE 10. First-time and experienced hosts

What differences do you notice between a dojo organizing your seminar for the first time and a dojo that has hosted you several times?

Possible probes:

- What do experienced hosts already know?
- What do first-time hosts commonly overlook?
- Could you give a specific comparison?

### CORE 11. How first-time organizers learn

When someone becomes responsible for organizing a seminar for the first time, how do they normally learn what needs to be done?

Possible probes:

- Written guidance?
- Previous documents?
- Checklists?
- Working with an experienced organizer?
- Verbal instructions?

### CORE 12. Where practical knowledge exists

Where does the practical knowledge about organizing a successful Aikido seminar mainly exist today?

Possible probes:

- Is it written down or held in the experience of senior people?
- How much is transferred through personal conversations?
- What could be difficult to recover if an experienced organizer stopped being involved?

### CORE 13. Current tools

From what you have observed, what tools do host dojos currently use to organize seminars and coordinate their teams?

Possible probes:

- Email?
- Messaging groups?
- Spreadsheets?
- Shared documents?
- Calendars?
- Paper notes?
- Event platforms?
- Which tool becomes the real source of truth?

### CORE 14. Previous process or tool change

Can you remember a recent time when a dojo or AWA introduced a new administrative tool or changed the way an event was organized?

Possible probes:

- What problem led to the change?
- Who proposed or approved it?
- Did people continue using it?
- What made adoption succeed or fail?

## 6. Additional questions

Use these only when they deepen a concrete example or cover a material gap.

1. **ADDITIONAL:** Can you recall a seminar where an important detail changed shortly before the event or after you had already arrived?
2. **ADDITIONAL:** Have you ever discovered that important information had not been communicated to you before a seminar?
3. **ADDITIONAL:** Can you remember a seminar where the main organizer became temporarily unavailable?
4. **ADDITIONAL:** Have you seen responsibilities transferred from an experienced organizer to someone with less experience?
5. **ADDITIONAL:** Across different host dojos, which organizational mistakes or misunderstandings repeat most often?
6. **ADDITIONAL:** When a seminar is poorly organized, how does that affect your ability to focus on teaching?
7. **ADDITIONAL:** What usually helps a host dojo recover effectively when an organizational problem occurs?
8. **ADDITIONAL:** Across the different countries and dojos you visit, which parts of seminar organization are generally the same?
9. **ADDITIONAL:** Which parts must remain specific to the host dojo, country, or local community?
10. **ADDITIONAL:** What organizational guidance or support does AWA currently provide?
11. **ADDITIONAL:** After a seminar is completed, how do host dojos preserve what they learned?
12. **ADDITIONAL:** Based on your experience, what would make it difficult for different Aikido dojos to use the same organizational process or system?

## 7. Neutral concept introduction

Only after the behavioral section is complete, read exactly:

> I would now like to briefly explain the direction I am exploring. I am considering an application that could help Aikido dojos organize seminars by keeping responsibilities, deadlines, important information, changes, and knowledge from previous events in one place. The concept is still at an early research stage.

From this point onward, label every response **[CF] Contaminated concept feedback**. These responses are concept reactions, not pure behavioral problem evidence, demand evidence, adoption evidence, or validated requirements.

Ask:

1. **[CF]** Based on your experience, which part of this concept addresses a real need, and which part may not be necessary?
2. **[CF]** What assumption might be incorrect?
3. **[CF]** What important part of seminar organization is missing from this description?
4. **[CF]** Under what circumstances could an organizational application create more work instead of reducing it?
5. **[CF]** Who within a host dojo would realistically use such a system?
6. **[CF]** What information should be visible to the visiting instructor?
7. **[CF]** What information should remain internal to the host dojo?
8. **[CF]** What would need to be true for dojos to adopt and continue using the system?
9. **[CF]** Which parts of organizing an Aikido seminar should remain based on personal communication and human judgment?
10. **[CF]** What results would show that such a system was genuinely successful?

## 8. Final questions

1. Is there an important aspect of organizing Aikido seminars that I have not asked about but should understand before developing anything?
2. Is there someone else with substantial experience in organizing Aikido seminars whom you believe I should speak with?
3. Would it be alright if I contact you again in the future if I need to clarify one or two points?

## 9. Closing script

> Sato Sensei, thank you very much for your time and for sharing your experience with me. Your answers have given me a much clearer understanding of how Aikido seminars are organized and where the real challenges may be. I will carefully review what I have learned before making decisions about the application. I truly appreciate your support.
