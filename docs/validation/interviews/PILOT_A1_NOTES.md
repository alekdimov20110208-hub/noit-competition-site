## 2. Problem evidence

### Current preparation process and roles

- Preparation for the martial-arts seminar initially involved approximately ten people.
- Responsibilities included social media, posts, videos, advertising, website maintenance, outreach to other clubs, participant communication, and transport.
- The participant recognized dependencies between tasks and people.

### Information storage and communication

- Information was distributed across personal notes, shared documents, cloud drives, chats, verbal communication, and individual memory.
- Some information existed only in people's memory.
- The participant reported that some information was duplicated across locations or people, but could not recall a concrete example.
- Backup plans sometimes existed, but they were recorded or communicated inconsistently.

### Delays, missed work, and replanning

- Tasks were sometimes completed late.
- Deadlines were missed.
- Some first attempts did not achieve the desired result.
- Last-minute changes regularly required additional work and replanning.

### Specific past incident: theatre activity

The participant described a concrete theatre incident with the following elements:

| Element | Grounded finding |
|---|---|
| Initial disruption | Participants withdrew before a rehearsal |
| People affected | Replacements were required |
| Dependent work affected | Scenery, clothing, and positions had to change |
| Additional constraint | Venue constraints were discovered during the final rehearsal |
| Consequence | Additional coordination, changes, and replanning were required |

This incident supports the existence of dependencies between people, resources, preparation tasks, and venue constraints in at least one real activity.

### Perceived value of preparation

- The participant considered a detailed structured plan valuable when it improves efficiency.
- No unprompted numeric answer was obtained for acceptable setup time.
- Acceptance of a 10–15-minute setup period was not measured.

## 3. Concept-reaction evidence

The following findings were recorded after discussion of the broad ReadyGraph concept. They are reactions to the proposed solution and positioning, not evidence that the underlying coordination problem is absent.

### Friction and update mechanism

- The current broad ReadyGraph concept was not accepted as sufficiently useful.
- The participant questioned how the system would learn that an element had failed.
- Manual status updates were viewed as friction.
- Requiring users to check another site was viewed as friction.
- For many situations, calling someone or using Viber and Google Drive was considered faster.

### Positioning and scope

- The participant compared the concept with established management tools.
- A product intended for all event types was considered insufficiently targeted.
- The participant recommended specializing in one event category, with martial-arts seminars and school theatre productions given as examples.
- A specialized event workspace was viewed as more promising than a generic dependency graph.

### Interpretation boundary

- The participant did not state that the underlying coordination problem was absent.
- The criticism concerned the proposed generic solution, its user effort, and its positioning.
- Because part of the concept discussion became a debate, spontaneous reactions must be distinguished from statements made later in response to the interviewer.

## 5. Interview limitations

- The interviewer suggested a percentage before the participant answered. The resulting frequency estimate is contaminated and must not be treated as reliable quantitative evidence.
- The required follow-up about whether 10–15 minutes would be acceptable was omitted.
- Some of the concept discussion became a debate. Spontaneous reactions should carry more evidential weight than later statements shaped by that exchange.
- The participant is relatively technically and product-oriented and must not be treated as representative of every organizer.
- This is one pilot interview. It cannot establish prevalence, market size, preferred specialization, or general willingness to adopt a product.

## 6. Evidence classification

### Supported by this interview at participant level

- A real organizing process involved multiple people and distinct responsibilities.
- Information was fragmented across several communication and storage methods.
- Missed or late work and last-minute changes caused additional effort and replanning.
- A specific past theatre incident involved cascading changes across people, scenery, clothing, positions, and venue constraints.
- The broad generic solution raised serious friction and positioning objections.

### Still hypotheses requiring more evidence

- How common these problems are across organizers and event categories.
- Whether martial-arts seminars or school theatre productions provide the stronger target direction.
- Whether a specialized workspace would be preferred over existing tools.
- Whether the relevant workflow can be modeled quickly enough.
- Whether useful status changes can be captured without burdensome manual updates.
- Whether a low-fidelity prototype reveals blockers more effectively than a normal checklist.
- Whether users would return to the product for later events.

## 7. Result of this interview

`REVISE`

The coordination problem remains under validation. The broad, all-event ReadyGraph solution is paused pending additional interviews and comparison of two specialized candidate directions.
