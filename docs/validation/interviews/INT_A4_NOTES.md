## Participant role and seminar context

- **[PR]** The participant was an experienced operational helper, not the chief organizer.
- **[PR]** The participant had helped organize recurring martial-arts seminars over multiple years.
- **[PR]** The recent event discussed was a domestic United States seminar with visitors from multiple states.
- **[PR]** Approximately five helpers were involved, excluding the chief instructor.
- **[PR]** Personal responsibilities included preparing breakfast for instructors and testing officials; preparing a testing or committee seating area; arranging tables, chairs, pens, and paper; helping with registration; welcoming and directing visitors; helping with printed signs and materials; informing visitors about meals and locations; closing the training venue; and supporting food and hospitality logistics.
- **[RI]** The participant has direct and independent relevance to seminar operations, but does not provide the complete strategic or chief-organizer perspective.

## Mature and repeatable workflow

- **[PR]** The participant had followed the seminar workflow repeatedly over multiple years.
- **[PR]** The workflow had become highly familiar and changed little between events.
- **[PR]** Repeated elements included instructor arrival, instructor food, registration setup, welcoming visitors, Friday training, food after training, breakfast, morning training, lunch, afternoon training, a group dinner, testing, and departure.
- **[PR]** Preparation did not feel especially intensive because the small recurring team already knew the routine.
- **[PR]** Almost every seminar followed the same process.
- **[PR]** The food provider was one of the few elements that might change.
- **[PR]** Food selection and venue cleanliness still required judgment.
- **[RI]** A reusable seminar template appears feasible for this workflow, but a mature team may stop consulting or updating a formal plan after the process becomes habitual.
- **[RI]** This interpretation applies only to the described team until tested elsewhere.

## Concrete incident: late breakfast

- **[PR]** A different helper was once responsible for delivering breakfast and arrived late.
- **[PO]** Timely breakfast was important because instructors needed time to eat, prepare, and warm up before training.
- **[PR]** The response was to stop assigning that responsibility to the unreliable person.
- **[RI]** The incident supports testing reliability history, clear ownership, time-sensitive tasks, and replacement of an unreliable owner.
- **[RI]** It does not show that software would necessarily have prevented the incident.

## Concrete incident: late food delivery

- **[PR]** Pizza delivery was sometimes late, and participants and instructors could see the delay.
- **[PR]** The team could order earlier, wait for the restaurant, or use a nearby alternative location if necessary.
- **[PR]** The team could not fully control the external delivery provider.
- **[RI]** The incident supports recording external-provider risk, timing windows, and simple fallback options.
- **[RI]** It also shows that not every risk requires a complex workflow or dependency model.

## Concrete incident: travel and testing disruption

- **[PR]** Flight cancellations caused some attendees to miss a seminar.
- **[PR]** The participant described those cancellations as outside the organizing team's direct control.
- **[PR]** In another situation, a person expected to participate in a rank test was late or unable to arrive.
- **[PR]** The testing schedule could be moved later or to another day when possible.
- **[PR]** If the participant could not arrive, the test could not take place.
- **[RI]** These incidents distinguish between recoverable delay and complete cancellation.
- **[RI]** The interview does not establish that the head instructor's flight was cancelled in the discussed seminar.

## Concrete incident: registration misunderstanding

- **[PR]** One attendee believed registration covered two seminars but had registered only for the first event.
- **[PR]** The missing registration was discovered when the attendee arrived at the later seminar.
- **[PR]** The team registered the attendee on site using a paper form.
- **[PR]** The consequence was limited and resolved manually.
- **[RI]** The incident supports testing clear event-specific registration status, participant confirmation, and a venue exception process.

## Helper administrative burden

- **[PO]** Helpers have work and personal responsibilities, limited time, and differing capacity for volunteer work.
- **[PO]** Helpers may not want administrative work and may become frustrated by preparation demands.
- **[PO]** Early communication and voluntary involvement are important.
- **[PO]** The person in charge should ask the wider group who can help, select people directly when volunteers do not respond, and communicate responsibilities in advance.
- **[RI]** This is strong participant-level evidence against heavy collaborator workflows.
- **[RI]** A candidate approach is to give helpers only minimal relevant information and a very lightweight confirmation action.

## Guided risks and dependencies

- **[PO]** Detailed prompts for every local transport failure did not appear useful to the participant because an unavailable driver in a major city could be replaced quickly with a ride-hailing service.
- **[PO]** A scenario involving an unconfirmed instructor flight did not match the participant's process because flights were normally confirmed well in advance.
- **[PO]** A cancellation or delay would have been a more relevant scenario.
- **[RI]** The participant did not provide clear support for a visual dependency graph or extensive guided risk prompts.
- **[RI]** Risk prompts, if tested, should be context-aware and focused on meaningful, non-obvious risks.
- **[RI]** The urban transport example must not be generalized to every seminar location.

## Interview-level decision

`REVISE`

The specialized seminar domain, repeatable workflow, and concentration of the complete picture in a senior organizer remain supported. However, current tools worked better than previously assumed, mature recurring teams may not need a permanent planning system, and helper administration must be extremely light. First-time or infrequent seminar organizers become a competing segment hypothesis rather than a selected target.

INT-A4 does not authorize application development or a final product decision. It supports moving the project to competitor and market research while preserving both candidate primary-user segments for comparison.
